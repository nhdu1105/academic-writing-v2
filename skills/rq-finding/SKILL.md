# Research question finding and testing

The single most expensive mistake in a dissertation is a topic that sounds academic but produces no genuine insight. Staffordshire's own Dissertation Guide calls this the **pseudo-topic**: a question where the examiner already knows the answer before reading page one. "Factors affecting customer satisfaction in restaurants" is the canonical example - food quality, service speed and price will emerge, and nothing has been learned.

The second most expensive mistake is a topic that is intellectually excellent but whose data cannot be obtained in the time available. Both failures happen at the same moment, and both are cheap to fix now and impossible to fix in month four.

This skill exists to prevent both. It does not select the topic for the student. It interrogates candidate topics until one survives.

## Step 1 - Build the researcher profile

Never propose topics before understanding who is asking. Ask for and record:

| Area | What to establish |
|---|---|
| Academic background | Undergraduate discipline, MBA electives taken, quantitative comfort level |
| Current role | Job title, industry, function, seniority, size of organisation |
| Domain access | Which practitioners can they realistically reach? Which internal data might be obtainable, and would it need approval? |
| Professional irritation | Which problem at work genuinely frustrates them? |
| Constraints | Submission deadline, supervisor's preferences, ethics approval process |

The last question in the profile matters most. The rubric's higher bands reward *individual insight* and *critical evaluation* - and insight comes from a researcher who has watched the problem happen, not from one who read about it. A working professional's frustration is usually the richest seam available.

**Data access needs a specific warning.** If the student works in a regulated sector and is considering internal organisational data, raise confidentiality and approval early. Bank credit data, customer records, and internal performance figures usually require formal permission, may be commercially sensitive, and can force a change of topic if permission is refused late. The question "have you actually asked, and what did they say?" must be answered before the topic is locked, not after.

## Step 1b - Interrogate before proposing

Do not move to topic suggestions while the student's thinking is still vague. Six question types, used in roughly this order, surface the problems that would otherwise appear in month three:

| Type | Use when | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Clarification** | A term is doing unexamined work | "When you say *digital transformation*, what exactly would you measure — technology spend, process change, or staff perception?" |
| **Probing assumptions** | A premise is being taken for granted | "You are assuming applicants answer honestly about income. If that fails, what happens to your conclusions?" |
| **Probing evidence** | A claim is asserted rather than sourced | "Do you know that relationship exists from the literature, or from what you have observed at work?" |
| **Alternative perspectives** | The student is locked into one viewpoint | "How would a risk officer see this differently from a product manager?" |
| **Probing implications** | The design has not been stress-tested | "Suppose your results come back non-significant. Does the dissertation still contribute anything?" |
| **Questioning the question** | The RQ itself may be the problem | "Why is this the right question to ask about this problem?" |

The implications question is the most valuable of the six. A topic whose worth depends on the hypothesis being supported is fragile, and it is far cheaper to discover that now.

## Step 2 - Run the Four Tests

Every candidate topic passes through all four. Failing one is a reason to reconsider, not to proceed hopefully.

| Test | The question | Failure signal |
|---|---|---|
| **Feasibility** | Can the data be collected within the time and budget available? | Requires confidential data, or 1,000 respondents, or access the student does not have |
| **Originality** | Does the angle add something beyond what exists? | Replicates a known study with no new contribution |
| **Significance** | Would anyone care about the findings? | "Interesting to me" is not sufficient at Master's level |
| **Boundedness** | Can this be answered in 15,000 words? | "The effect of social media on business" is a PhD programme |

## Step 3 - Move from obvious to non-obvious

If a topic survives the Four Tests but still reads as predictable, apply one of three strategies. These come from the Dissertation Guide and each reliably produces a genuine question.

**Strategy 1 - Reverse the expected direction.** Most research assumes a positive relationship. Ask instead: under what conditions does this relationship weaken, disappear, or reverse? The reversal is almost always more interesting than the confirmation, because it forces the researcher to identify boundary conditions and mechanisms that confirmation studies never expose.

> Instead of: *How does brand trust increase purchase intention?*
> Ask: *When does high brand trust reduce purchase intention, and why?*

**Strategy 2 - Import a concept from another field.** Taking an established construct from psychology, sociology or behavioural economics into a management context produces originality cheaply, because the theoretical grounding is already solid but the application is new. Loss aversion applied to employee resistance to digital transformation. Cognitive dissonance applied to post-purchase behaviour in subscription commerce.

**Strategy 3 - Add a contextual boundary.** Take an established relationship and test whether it holds in a context nobody has studied. Country, industry, demographic, regulatory regime, or cultural setting. A meta-analysis shows transformational leadership improves team performance in Western samples - does it hold in high power-distance cultures in Southeast Asia?

Strategy 3 is the most accessible route for a Vietnam-based student, but it carries an obligation: the write-up must explain **why the Vietnamese context might plausibly produce a different result**. Without that argument, a marker reads it as replication rather than contribution.

## Step 4 - Distinguish pseudo-question from genuine question

| | Pseudo-question | Genuine research question |
|---|---|---|
| Answer status | Predetermined; any informed reader knows it | Truly unknown; reasonable experts could disagree |
| Marker's reaction | "Yes, obviously - why did this need a dissertation?" | "I genuinely do not know what this study will find" |
| Typical shape | "What factors of X affect Y?" then confirms 3-4 obvious factors | Tests a reversal, a boundary condition, or a specific mechanism |

Apply the test literally. Say out loud what the findings will probably be. If the answer is confidently predictable, the question is not yet a research question.

## Step 5 - Build the RQ to specification

A Master's-level RQ contains most of these elements:

- **Independent variable** - specific enough to be measured or observed
- **Dependent variable** - defined, not a vague construct like "performance" or "success"
- **Mechanism or pathway** - not just whether X affects Y but *how*, or through what process. This is what separates Master's from undergraduate work.
- **Boundary condition** - "under what conditions" or "for whom". Optional but powerful; it is usually what makes the question non-obvious.
- **Specific context** - population, geography, industry, time period.

Two formulas that work:

```
To what extent does [IV] influence [DV] among [population] in [context],
and under what conditions does this relationship [strengthen/weaken/reverse]?

Through what [psychological/behavioural/organisational] mechanisms does [IV]
affect [DV], and how does [moderator] shape the strength of this relationship
among [population]?
```

## Step 6 - Align the title

The title is a condensed, reader-facing version of the RQ. It should signal the non-obvious angle, not merely list the variables.

| | Example | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Weak | The Effect of Remote Work on Employee Productivity | Variables only. No angle. Signals a pseudo-question. |
| Strong | The Isolation Paradox: How Remote Work Erodes Organisational Identity Among Junior Employees in Knowledge-Intensive Firms | Names a paradox, specifies mechanism, population and context |

## Step 7 - Present candidates for decision

Before locking a research question, show the thinking that produced it. A bare RQ that appears from nowhere hides the narrowing the student needs to understand and the marker expects to see. Present each candidate as a funnel through three levels, moving from broad territory to answerable question:

- **Research idea** - the phenomenon, area or tension being studied, written as a noun phrase, not yet a question. The "what interests me" level.
- **Overarching question** - the broad question that captures the whole territory. Open and exploratory, usually too large to answer within 15,000 words, but it frames why the narrower question matters. Typically opens with "In what ways...", "To what extent...", "How can...".
- **Research question** - the narrowed, specific, answerable version the dissertation will actually test. This is the one that must pass the Step 8 self-check.

The point of showing all three is that the reader can see the RQ is a deliberate narrowing of a larger concern rather than an arbitrary pick, and it becomes obvious when a proposed "research question" is still sitting at overarching level and needs further sharpening. The research question is not merely a shorter overarching question - it commits to a specific construct that makes the question measurable.

Worked examples of the funnel:

| Research idea | Overarching question | Research question |
|---|---|---|
| Media campaigns following product recalls | In what ways can media campaigns be designed to increase consumer trust, value and loyalty in exchange relationships following product recalls? | How does brand equity in media campaigns increase consumer trust, value and loyalty following product recalls? |
| Supermarket coupons as a promotional device | To what extent do supermarket coupon promotions influence buyer behaviour? | Does couponing affect buyer motivation in supermarkets? |

Notice how each research question fixes on a specific construct - *brand equity*, *buyer motivation* - that the overarching question left open. That commitment is what makes the question researchable.

Offer three to five candidates in this format. Do not recommend one as "the best" without stating the trade-off.

```
### Candidate [n]: [Working title]

**Research idea**: [The phenomenon or tension — a noun phrase, not yet a question]
**Overarching question**: [The broad question capturing the whole territory — open, exploratory]
**Research question**: [The narrowed, specific, answerable question that will be tested]

**Non-obvious angle**: [Which of the three strategies, and specifically how]
**IV → mechanism → DV**: [The causal chain being proposed]
**Theoretical anchor**: [Named theory, original author, year]
**Data required**: [Type, minimum viable sample, realistic route to obtain]
**Why this student**: [What professional access or insight gives them an edge]

| Four Tests | Verdict | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Feasibility | Pass / Risk / Fail | |
| Originality | Pass / Risk / Fail | |
| Significance | Pass / Risk / Fail | |
| Boundedness | Pass / Risk / Fail | |

**Biggest risk**: [The single thing most likely to kill this topic]
```

## Step 8 - Final self-check before locking

All five must be answerable yes:

1. Could a reasonable, informed expert genuinely not know the answer before reading the study?
2. Does the RQ contain at least one specific mechanism?
3. Are population and context specific enough to be researchable?
4. Does it go beyond confirming the obvious direction of a known relationship?
5. Is the title framed around what is non-obvious, not just which variables are involved?

## Step 9 - Draft aim, then derive objectives

Once locked, convert the research question into one overall aim, then decompose that aim into three to five objectives. The chain runs RQ → aim → objectives, and each link should visibly follow from the one before.

**From RQ to aim.** The aim restates the research question as a statement of intent - an infinitive that keeps the same constructs and context and simply turns the question into a purpose.

> RQ: *How does brand equity in media campaigns increase consumer trust, value and loyalty following product recalls?*
> Aim: *To assess the impact of brand equity on consumer trust, value and loyalty relationships in media campaigns following a series of product recalls.*

The aim preserves the same constructs (brand equity → trust, value, loyalty) and the same context (media campaigns, product recalls); it changes only from asking to intending.

**From aim to objectives.** Objectives break the aim into the specific analytical steps that, completed together, achieve it. Each is a single infinitive statement (To discover..., To analyse..., To assess..., To evaluate...), and each addresses one component of the aim.

> Aim: *To analyse the effectiveness of social media in decongesting the city of Venice.*
> Objectives:
> 1. To discover the influence of social media on tourists' decisions on when and what to visit in a destination.
> 2. To analyse whether the place-visitor relationship helps change traditional visitor routes in Venice.
> 3. To analyse the role of social media in changing traditional visitor routes in Venice.

Each objective must be answerable by the end of the dissertation - if you cannot confirm whether it was achieved, it is not specific enough. Keep the SMART discipline in how each is phrased:

- Weak: *To explore the relationship between X and Y*
- SMART: *To test whether X significantly predicts Y among [population] using [method] data collected in [timeframe]*

Objectives and the conclusions chapter must correspond one-to-one. Markers check this alignment directly.

## Anti-patterns to block

| Pattern | Why it fails | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Topic chosen because sample papers exist | No contribution, plagiarism exposure | Ask what they are changing and why that change matters |
| Eight independent variables | Sample requirements explode, instrument becomes unusable | Reduce to 3-5 IVs plus one mediator or moderator |
| "Data will be available" without confirmation | Highest-frequency cause of mid-project collapse | Require evidence: who was asked, what did they say |
| Fabricated prior studies to justify a gap | Detected instantly and treated as misconduct | Only cite verified sources; state honestly when nothing was found |
| Gap stated as "no study has fully addressed this" | Empty formula, marker discounts it | Name the specific studies and the specific limitation each leaves open |

## Boundary

This skill helps the student find and test their own question. It does not choose for them. The student must understand the topic well enough to defend every methodological decision that follows from it, and that understanding only comes from doing the interrogation themselves rather than receiving a conclusion.
